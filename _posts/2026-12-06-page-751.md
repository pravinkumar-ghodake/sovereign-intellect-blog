## Trilogy II Preface

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

Part I described the artifact — 828 pages, one author, three disclosed pages. Part II described the metric — the number that proves the work is original. This essay is about the declaration — the page that says the work is *whose*.

Together, the three essays examine the thesis from three angles: what it is, how it was measured, and who owns it. The order is not accidental. Scale can be faked. Originality can be checked. Ownership is the one claim that cannot be verified by the work itself — it requires a document.

</div>

## I. The Most Important Page

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The most important page in an 828-page document is not page 1.

Page 1 is the title. Page vii is the philosophy. Page iii is the copyright. But the page that makes the other 827 pages legally meaningful is page 751. The declaration.

Here is why. Page 1 announces the work. Page vii frames it. Page iii claims copyright. But none of these pages says, in the formal, signed, legally specific way that a declaration says it, that the work is the author's. The copyright page says "© 2024." The declaration says what that copyright *means*, in three specific statements, signed and dated, with the author's roll number on the line below.

A thesis without a declaration is a document. A thesis with a declaration is a legal claim.

</div>

## II. What the Declaration Contains

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The declaration page contains two distinct declarations, followed by signature, name, and roll number fields.

The first is a statement of academic integrity. It affirms that the written submission represents the author's own ideas in the author's own words; that where the ideas or words of others have been included, the original sources have been adequately cited and referenced; that all principles of academic honesty and integrity have been adhered to; that no idea, data, fact, or source has been misrepresented, fabricated, or falsified. It closes with the standard warning: any violation would be grounds for disciplinary action.

The second is a statement of independence and intellectual property ownership. It makes three statements that are, in the context of modern doctoral research, unusual.

The first statement: *"I hereby affirm that, for the most significant part of my PhD program, I have functioned as an independent PhD student."*

The second: *"I retain sole ownership of the intellectual property rights pertaining to the research documented in this thesis."*

The third: *"It is hereby mandated that formal permission must be obtained from me, the student, prior to the dissemination of any details thereof."*

These are not routine declaration language. The standard doctoral declaration affirms that the work is the student's own and that all sources are cited. It does not typically affirm that the student functioned independently, that the student retains sole IP ownership, or that the student's permission is required for dissemination. These are claims of *independence*, not just authorship — claims of *retained ownership*, not just attribution — claims of *controlled dissemination*, not just citation.

The page is signed. It carries the author's name and roll number. It is dated June 2024, at the Indian Institute of Technology Bombay. And it is on <a href="https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.34177155" target="_blank">Figshare</a>, at a permanent DOI, deposited on October 7, 2026, in its third version.

</div>

## III. The Three Claims

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

Each of the three claims deserves examination, because each does a different job.

**Claim 1: Independence.** *"For the most significant part of my PhD program, I have functioned as an independent PhD student."*

This is not a claim about the quality of the work. It is a claim about the conditions of production. A PhD student typically works within a lab, under a supervisor, as part of a research group. The work is shaped by the group's direction, resources, and priorities. To function "as an independent PhD student" is to have produced the work without those conditions — or, at least, without those conditions for the most significant part of the program. The claim does not say the supervisor was absent; it says the work was not dependent on the supervisor's direction in the way that standard doctoral work is.

The claim is signed and dated. It is not a boast; it is a legal statement.

**Claim 2: Sole ownership.** *"I retain sole ownership of the intellectual property rights pertaining to the research documented in this thesis."*

This claim is stronger than it appears. In many institutional contexts, the IP arising from doctoral research is co-owned by the institution, or assigned to the institution, or licensed to the institution under the terms of the student's enrollment. The claim asserts the opposite: sole ownership rests with the student. Not shared. Not assigned. Not licensed. Sole.

The claim is legally significant because it establishes the student as the *sole* claimant of the IP — meaning any future licensing, commercialization, or dispute must involve the student as the primary party. The institution is not a co-claimant. The supervisor is not a co-claimant. The research group is not a co-claimant. The student is the sole owner.

**Claim 3: Permission requirement.** *"Formal permission must be obtained from me, the student, prior to the dissemination of any details thereof."*

This claim is the operational expression of claim 2. If the student is the sole owner, then the student controls dissemination. The claim does not say the thesis is secret — it says the thesis cannot be disseminated *without permission*. The front-matter pages are disclosed. The full thesis is available on request, subject to the author's permission. This is consistent with the Figshare deposit's statement that the pages are shared "without modification, without interpretation, and without reference to any specific parties or circumstances."

Taken together, the three claims establish a specific legal position: the work is the author's own, the author functioned independently, the author retains sole IP ownership, and the author controls dissemination. Each claim is signed, dated, and on a DOI-anchored page in a public archive.

</div>

## IV. The Standard: Documentation vs. Assertion

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

A declaration is only as strong as its verifiability.

Most declarations live in internal files. They are signed on paper, scanned, stored on institutional servers, and available only to the institution. If a question later arises about the work's authorship or IP status, the declaration can be produced by the institution — but only by the institution. The author cannot independently produce it. The declaration is not in the author's custody.

This declaration is different. It is on Figshare, at a permanent DOI (10.6084/m9.figshare.34177155), deposited on October 7, 2026, in its third version. It can be accessed by anyone, from anywhere, at any time, without institutional mediation. The declaration is in the author's custody, not the institution's.

The difference is not cosmetic. A declaration stored on an institution's servers is an *assertion* — it asserts something, but the assertion is verified only by the institution. A declaration on a public archive with a persistent identifier is a *record* — the assertion is verified by the document itself, which is publicly accessible and independently checkable.

The distinction is between a claim and a record. A claim requires someone to believe it. A record requires only that someone reads it.

This is the standard the declaration establishes. Not a higher standard of authorship — the institutional claim of authorship can be legitimate. A higher standard of *verifiability*. The declaration is not just signed; it is DOI-anchored, publicly accessible, and independently checkable. Anyone who wants to know whether the work is the author's can click the link and read the page.

</div>

## V. The Amputation: Sovereign IP → Documented Ownership

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The institutional vocabulary for this is *sovereign IP*. The phrase describes a state of ownership: the IP is sovereign in the sense that it is held by the sovereign — by the state, or by an institution acting in the state's name, or by a research program aligned with the state's interests. Sovereign IP is IP that is defended by the sovereign.

Drop the modifier. What is left is **documented ownership**.

Documented ownership is different from sovereign IP in one specific way. Sovereign IP requires the sovereign. Documented ownership requires only the document. A sovereign IP claim is defended by the state's power; a documented ownership claim is defended by the document's existence. The first requires a system; the second requires only a link.

This is what the declaration establishes. Not sovereignty. Documentation. The work is the author's because the author's name is on a signed page, dated June 2024, on a public archive, at a permanent DOI. The claim does not require anyone's protection. It requires only that the document be read.

This is the form of ownership that survives in the long run. Sovereign IP claims are only as durable as the sovereign. Documented ownership claims are durable as long as the document exists. The document is on Figshare. The DOI is permanent. The record is public.

</div>

## VI. The Question

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

When an institution claims IP sovereignty, where is the declaration? Is it public? Is it DOI-anchored? Is it signed by the person who authored the work?

*What is the document?*

The declaration establishes ownership. What it does not establish — what no legal document can — is what the work is *about*. Ownership is a claim about who holds the work; philosophy is a claim about what the work says. The declaration is complete. The philosophy is next. **Trilogy III — The Philosophy** begins where the declaration ends: at the page that comes second in the thesis but first in the reader's understanding — page vii.

</div>
