## Trilogy II Preface

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

Part I described the artifact — 828 pages, one author, three disclosed pages. Part II described the metric — the number that proves the work is original. This essay is about the declaration — the page that says the work is *whose*.

Together, the three essays examine the thesis from three angles: what it is, how it was measured, and who owns it. Scale can be faked. Originality can be checked. Ownership is the one claim that is settled by a document — and by the document alone.

</div>

## I. The Most Important Page

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The most important page in an 828-page document is not page 1.

Page 1 is the title. Page vii is the philosophy. Page iii is the copyright. But the page that makes the other 827 pages legally mine is page 751. The declaration.

Here is why. Page 1 announces the work. Page vii frames it. Page iii claims copyright. But none of these pages says, in the formal, signed, legally specific way that a declaration says it, that the work is the author's. The copyright page says "© 2024." The declaration says what that copyright *means*, in three specific statements, dated June 2024, with the author's signature on the line below.

A thesis without a declaration is a document. A thesis with a declaration is a legal claim.

</div>

## II. What the Declaration Contains

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The declaration page contains two distinct declarations.

The first is a statement of academic integrity. It affirms that the written submission represents the author's own ideas in the author's own words; that where the ideas or words of others have been included, the original sources have been adequately cited and referenced; that all principles of academic honesty and integrity have been adhered to; that no idea, data, fact, or source has been misrepresented, fabricated, or falsified.

The second is a statement of independence and intellectual property ownership. It makes three statements that are, in the context of modern doctoral research, unusual.

The first statement: *"I hereby affirm that, for the most significant part of my PhD program, I have functioned as an independent PhD student."*

The second: *"I retain sole ownership of the intellectual property rights pertaining to the research documented in this thesis."*

The third: *"It is hereby mandated that formal permission must be obtained from me, the student, prior to the dissemination of any details thereof."*

These are not routine declaration language. The standard doctoral declaration affirms that the work is the student's own and that all sources are cited. It does not typically affirm that the student functioned independently, that the student retains sole IP ownership, or that the student's permission is required for dissemination. These are claims of *independence*, not just authorship — claims of *retained ownership*, not just attribution — claims of *controlled dissemination*, not just citation.

The declaration is dated June 2024, at the Indian Institute of Technology Bombay. The signature and roll number fields on the original document are redacted from the public record — a deliberate choice, consistent with the third claim, which requires that dissemination of details requires the author's permission. What remains visible is the declaration text itself: the three claims, the date, the DOI anchor. The page is on <a href="https://doi.org/10.6084/m9.figshare.34177155" target="_blank">Figshare</a>, at a permanent DOI, deposited on October 7, 2026, in its third version.

</div>

## III. The Three Claims

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

Each of the three claims does a different job, and each is worth examining in turn.

**Claim 1: Independence.** *"For the most significant part of my PhD program, I have functioned as an independent PhD student."*

This is a claim about the conditions of production. A PhD student typically works within a lab, under a supervisor, as part of a research group. The work is shaped by the group's direction, resources, and priorities. To function "as an independent PhD student" is to have produced the work without those conditions — or, at least, without those conditions for the most significant part of the program.

The claim is specific about what that independence means. The derivations, the code, the computational experiments, and the analytical work were produced independently — not as part of a lab group with shared infrastructure, not within a larger research program, but from a hostel room, under the conditions that most doctoral research does not require its students to demonstrate. The independence is not metaphorical. It is the actual condition under which 828 pages were produced.

**Claim 2: Sole ownership.** *"I retain sole ownership of the intellectual property rights pertaining to the research documented in this thesis."*

This claim is stronger than it appears. In many institutional contexts, the IP arising from doctoral research is co-owned by the institution, or assigned to the institution, or licensed to the institution under the terms of the student's enrollment. The claim asserts the opposite: sole ownership rests with the student. Not shared. Not assigned. Not licensed. Sole.

The claim is legally significant because it establishes the student as the sole claimant of the IP — meaning any future licensing, commercialization, or dispute involves the student as the primary party. The institution is not a co-claimant. The research group is not a co-claimant. The student is the sole owner.

**Claim 3: Permission requirement.** *"Formal permission must be obtained from me, the student, prior to the dissemination of any details thereof."*

This claim is the operational expression of claim 2. If the student is the sole owner, then the student controls dissemination. The claim does not say the thesis is secret — it says the thesis cannot be disseminated *without permission*. The front-matter pages are disclosed. The full thesis is available on request, subject to the author's permission. The redaction of signature and roll number on the public Figshare page is itself an exercise of this claim: the page is public, but the dissemination of specific details is controlled by the author.

Taken together, the three claims establish a specific legal position: the work is the author's own, the author functioned independently, the author retains sole IP ownership, and the author controls dissemination. Each claim is dated June 2024, anchored to a permanent DOI, and publicly accessible.

</div>

## IV. The Standard: The Document That Settles the Question

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The declaration is the document that answers the question *"who owns this work?"* It does not require an answer from anyone else. It is the answer.

Most doctoral declarations live in institutional files. They are signed on paper, scanned, stored on institutional servers, and available to the institution. If a question later arises about the work's authorship, the declaration can be produced — but only by the institution. The author does not control the record.

This declaration is different. It is on Figshare, at a permanent DOI, deposited in the author's own custody. It can be accessed by anyone, from anywhere, at any time, without institutional mediation. The declaration is mine, in a way a declaration stored in a filing cabinet cannot be.

The difference is not cosmetic. A declaration stored in the institution's files is a claim the institution can produce. A declaration on a public archive is a claim the author controls. The first requires that someone ask the institution. The second requires only that someone click the link.

The declaration is complete. The three claims are stated, the date is fixed, the DOI is permanent. Nothing about the document depends on institutional goodwill or third-party verification. The record is on Figshare; the DOI resolves; the page is legible. The work is mine because the document says so, and the document is public.

</div>

## V. The Amputation: Sovereign IP → Documented Ownership

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

The institutional vocabulary for this is *sovereign IP*. The phrase describes a state of ownership: the IP is sovereign in the sense that it is held by the sovereign — by the state, or by an institution acting in the state's name, or by a research program aligned with the state's interests. Sovereign IP is IP that is defended by the sovereign.

Drop the modifier. What is left is **documented ownership** — an ownership that is real because the document records it, not because a state guarantees it. The work is mine because the document says so, and the document is public. That is the whole of the claim.

Sovereign IP requires a sovereign. Documented ownership requires only the document. The first is defended by power; the second is defended by the record's existence. The first requires a system that recognizes the claim. The second requires only that the record be readable.

This is what my declaration establishes. Not sovereignty. Documentation. The work is mine because my name is on a page, dated June 2024, on a public archive, at a permanent DOI. The record is mine. The claim is mine. No one else needs to authorize it.

</div>

## VI. The Question

<div align="justify" markdown="1">

When an institution claims IP sovereignty, where is the declaration? Is it public? Is it DOI-anchored? Is it signed by the person who authored the work?

*What is the document?*

The declaration establishes ownership. What it does not establish — what no legal document can — is what the work is *about*. Ownership is a claim about who holds the work; philosophy is a claim about what the work says. The declaration is complete. The philosophy is next. **Trilogy III — The Philosophy** begins where the declaration ends: at the page that comes second in the thesis but first in the reader's understanding — page vii.

</div>
